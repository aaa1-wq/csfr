## The Question (0:00–0:45)

Let me put one question on the table: **if every "evil" business died tomorrow — the polluters, the price-gougers, the sweatshops — would we, the customers, actually let it? Or would we quietly bring them back?**

Most conversations about corporate ethics put the company on trial. They ask whether this industry is glorious or harmful, whether this leader is greedy, whether that firm deserves our outrage. But the person who keeps the villain in business is rarely the villain. It's us. So I want to hold the customer up to the light today — because **an evil company doesn't survive on evil alone. It survives on our repeat purchases.**

**中文：** 我先抛一个问题：**如果所有“邪恶”公司明天就死掉——排污的、坐地起价的、血汗工厂——我们这些消费者，真的会允许吗？还是会悄悄把它们请回来？**

关于企业伦理的讨论，大多是在审判公司：这个行业是辉煌还是有害、这个领导贪不贪、那家公司配不配被骂。但真正养活着“反派”的人，几乎从不是反派自己，而是我们。所以今天我想把“消费者”放到灯下——因为**一家邪恶公司，从来不是靠“恶”活着的，而是靠我们的回头客。**

---

## Why the Customer Holds the Knife (0:45–2:15)

Think about how an evil company actually pays its bills. The tobacco industry survived for half a century — not because it fooled everyone (the health warnings were public for decades), but because people kept buying. Fast fashion runs on waste and underpaid labor because the alternative costs three times as much and we'd rather not pay it. Purdue sold OxyContin for twenty years because doctors prescribed it and patients asked for it by name.

None of them held a gun to anyone's head. We chose them — often for reasons that felt reasonable at the time: the moral option was slower, pricier, or just less convenient. And that's the uncomfortable part. The moral weight of a business doesn't rest only in the boardroom. It rests every time we swipe a card. **When we complain about a greedy company while still buying its product, we're voting for it with our wallet.**

The villain story is comforting because it lets us stay the audience — safely on the right side of the screen. But we're not the audience. We're the market, and the market is the only thing the company was ever really serving.

**中文：** 想想一家邪恶公司到底靠什么付账。烟草业活了半个世纪——不是因为它骗过了所有人（健康警告公开了几十年），而是因为人们一直买。快时尚靠浪费和低薪运转，因为替代品贵三倍，我们不愿多付。普渡卖了二十年奥施康定，因为医生开方、病人点名要。

他们谁也没拿枪顶着谁的头。是我们选了它——理由在当时听起来还挺合理：更有道德的那个更慢、更贵、或者就是不方便。这才是扎人的地方。企业的道德重量，不光在会议室里，也在我们每一次刷卡时。**当我们一边骂贪婪的公司、一边继续买它的东西，我们就是在用钱包给它投票。**

反派叙事之所以舒服，是因为它能让我们留在“观众席”——安全地站在屏幕正确的一边。但我们不是观众，我们是市场，而市场才是企业真正服务的唯一对象。

## The Real Case — Tobacco (2:15–3:05)

If you want proof that an "evil" business can run for a century on our repeat purchases, look at tobacco. This isn't opinion — it went to court and was ruled on.

In 1994, the heads of the seven largest tobacco companies testified before the US Congress, under oath, that **nicotine was not addictive.** Internal documents later proved the opposite: the companies knew nicotine was addictive, studied exactly how to deliver it more efficiently, and adjusted the chemistry of cigarettes to make them more addictive — while publicly denying it. In 2006, after a nine-month trial, US District Judge Gladys Kessler found the major tobacco companies **guilty of racketeering** — of a decades-long conspiracy to defraud the public about the dangers of smoking and the addictiveness of nicotine. The ruling said the companies had "marketed and sold their lethal product with zeal, with deception, with a single-minded focus on their financial success, and without regard for the human tragedy or social costs that success exacted."

Think about that. A product that kills about half its long-term users, sold by companies that knew it was addictive and lied about it — and it is still, today, one of the most profitable consumer businesses on earth. Why? Not because the deception was perfect. Because **we kept buying.** That's the point I want to land: a company can be proven evil in a court of law, and the market will quietly keep it alive as long as the product keeps selling.

*Sources: US v. Philip Morris (D.D.C. 2006, Judge Gladys Kessler) — see [DOJ case materials](https://www.justice.gov/civil/cases/tobacco2) and [UCSF Industry Documents Library](https://www.industrydocuments.ucsf.edu/resources/litigation-background); 1994 congressional testimony and the internal "nicotine" documents are archived there.*

**中文：** 如果你想找证据，看烟草——这不是观点，而是法院判过的。

1994 年，美国七大烟草公司的负责人在国会**宣誓作证**说“尼古丁不具成瘾性”。后来的内部文件证明恰恰相反：公司早就知道尼古丁会成瘾，还专门研究如何更高效地递送它、调整香烟的化学配方让它更上瘾——同时公开否认。2006 年，经过九个月庭审，美国地区法官格拉迪斯·凯斯勒（Gladys Kessler）判定几家大烟草公司**犯有诈骗罪（racketeering）**——它们策划了长达数十年的阴谋，欺骗公众对吸烟危害和尼古丁成瘾性的认知。判决书写道，这些公司“以极大的热情、以欺骗、以对财务成功的执念来营销和销售他们的致命产品，全然不顾这一成功所付出的人类悲剧与社会代价”。

想一想：一种会让约一半长期使用者丧命的产品，卖它的公司明知会上瘾还撒谎——而它至今仍是全球最赚钱的消费品生意之一。为什么？不是因为骗术天衣无缝，而是因为**我们一直在买。** 这正是我想落到的点：一家公司可以在法庭上被证明是“恶”的，只要产品还卖得动，市场就会悄悄让它活下去。

## The Hard Test (2:15–3:35)

So let's run an honest test. Imagine every evil company vanished tonight. What would actually happen tomorrow? Would we celebrate — or would the first thing many of us do be to hunt for a cheaper replacement?

Watch what happens after a scandal. A brand's stock tanks, and bargain-hunters move in. When a company gets exposed, our first instinct is often not to switch — it's to check whether the price just went *down.* That's the tell. **In principle we want ethical business; in practice we want cheap business.** And companies know it, which is exactly why "do the minimum that keeps customers" so often beats "do the most good."

This is also why boycotts rarely last. We punish a company with our attention for a week, then go back for the discount. The company learns the real lesson: outrage is cheap, and loyalty is cheaper.

**中文：** 那我们做一个诚实的测试。设想今晚所有邪恶公司都消失了。明早到底会怎样？我们会庆祝吗——还是多数人第一件事，就是去找更便宜的替代品？

看看丑闻之后会发生什么。品牌股价暴跌，捡便宜的人就进场。一家公司被曝光，我们的第一反应往往不是“换掉”，而是看看价格是不是掉了。这就是破绽。**原则上我们要有道德的企业，实际上我们要便宜的企业。**而公司太懂这一点了——这正是“只做留住客户的最低限度”常常打败“最多的善”的原因。

这也是抵制很少持久的原因。我们用注意力惩罚它一周，然后为了折扣又回来。公司学到真正的教训是：愤怒很廉价，忠诚更廉价。

## The Counterpoint — and Why It Still Matters (3:35–4:25)

Now, I have to be fair, because this argument can go wrong. Saying "the customer keeps the evil company alive" is *not* the same as saying the customer is to blame for the harm. That would be too easy, and it lets the company off the hook. A factory still chose to poison the river; demand doesn't justify poisoning it. The company owns its decisions.

But here's the point I actually want to make: even if the company is guilty, **change never happens through guilt alone.** Regulation helps, better leaders help — but they're slow and they leak. The one lever that moves instantly, every single day, is demand. Every dollar is a vote, and unlike a real election, this one you cast dozens of times a week.

So the real question isn't "are companies evil?" It's "**are we willing to pay the price of our own values?**" Because that's the only part of the equation we fully control.

**中文：** 现在我必须公道一点，因为这个论证容易走偏。说“是消费者养活了邪恶公司”，**不等于**说“消费者该为伤害负责”。那太容易了，也替企业开脱了。工厂毕竟是它自己选择往河里排污的；需求不能成为排污的理由。决定是公司的。

但我想说的重点是：就算公司有罪，**光靠愧疚感，改变永远不会发生。**监管有用、更好的领导有用——可它们都慢，而且会漏。那个能瞬间撬动、且每天都在起作用的杠杆，是“需求”。每一块钱都是一票，而且跟真正的选举不同，这张票你一周要投几十次。

所以真问题不是“公司坏不坏”，而是**“我们愿不愿意为自己的价值观买单？”**因为这正是整个等式里，我们唯一能完全掌控的那一项。

---

## Closing (4:25–5:00)

So my answer to the question I opened with — would we let the evil company die? — is: **not until we stop being the reason it lives.**

The most powerful ESG policy in the world isn't a regulation or a mission statement. It's a buyer who pays for their beliefs — and keeps walking away.

So the next time you reach for the cheaper, faster, less ethical option — and we all do — **ask yourself whose business you're really keeping alive.**

**中文：** 所以对我最初那个问题的回答——我们会允许邪恶公司死去吗？——是：**除非我们不再当它活着的理由，否则不会。**

世界上最有力量的 ESG 政策，不是法规，也不是使命宣言，而是一个为自己的信念买单、并且一直转身离开的消费者。

所以下次你伸手去拿那个更便宜、更快、更不道德的选择时——我们都会——**问问自己，你到底在养活谁的生意。**
