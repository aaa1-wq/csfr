# Business: Glorious or Evil? — Full Spoken Script
### Theme 5 · 40-minute guided discussion (19 slides)

## Slide 1 — Cover (00:00–01:30)

Good morning, everyone. Before we start, I want you to picture one thing.

An engine. A big, powerful engine, sitting on the floor between us. Now — that same engine could drive an ambulance, or it could drive a tank. The machine doesn't care. It doesn't have a personality. It just amplifies whatever it's pointed at and whatever it's told to do.

That's business. It's not good, and it's not evil. It's an engine. But the steering — the *pointing* — that's human. And that's what we're going to talk about for the next forty minutes.

The question on the screen is the one I want you to hold the whole time: **is business more good than bad?** Don't answer yet. Just hold it.

---

## Slide 2 — Five questions, one spine (01:30–03:00)

Here's the road. Five questions — and I want to be clear from the start: these are not five separate topics. They're one story, told in five moves.

We start out *there*, in the world of companies — with the shock of seeing good and evil in the very same industry. Then we climb. Is it, on balance, net good? Would you factor ethics into your own job hunt? If you ever sit in the leader's chair, will you choose profit or purpose? And we end on a question that sounds almost silly — shopping malls — but quietly asks whether the whole institution of business survives.

Notice the arc: it begins far away, in boardrooms and movies, and it ends right here, in your own choices. Let's go.

## Slide 3 — Part 1 divider (03:00–04:00)

Part one. Glorious or evil.

Most of us already carry an image of business as a machine that grinds people up. This is that image — Charlie Chaplin, in *Modern Times*, literally caught in the gears. [point to screen] That's the picture a hundred years of film has given us.

Hold it. In about two minutes, I'm going to set another image beside it. From the exact same industry.

## Slide 4 — Same industry, two headlines (04:00–07:00)

Let's make this concrete. One industry: pharmaceuticals.

On the left — look at that. A tractor in a wheat field, the machinery of modern agriculture — because pharma and agribusiness both show the same split. But stay with pharma. The glory: mRNA vaccines compressed about a decade of research into a single year. Smallpox is gone. Polio is nearly gone. Profit, here, is fuel — the returns fund the next generation of cures.

Now the right side. Same industry. Purdue's OxyContin — a drug that was marketed as safe, and that contributed to nearly six hundred and forty-five thousand overdose deaths in the US between 1999 and 2021. The EpiPen — a device that costs about a dollar to make — jumping more than six hundred percent, with no real competition to stop it. The playbook: capture the regulator, patent a lifeline, and price the vulnerable.

Same industry. Both of those are true. So the honest question isn't "which industry is evil." It's "which *decisions* were made — and by whom."

## Slide 5 — How film frames the machine (07:00–09:00)

And films give us the two stories we tell about those decisions.

On the left, *Modern Times*: business is evil, businessmen have no heart, workers are machines. The fear is that efficiency swallows the human being whole.

In the middle, the same engine — two stories. On the right, *Silicon Valley*: a small team builds something they genuinely love, and a new CEO wants to turn it into a boxed product for enterprises. Growth versus soul, on one whiteboard. The hope is that the small can still choose.

Here's the question I want to ask you: **which is scarier — a villain, or a good person doing a questionable thing because the numbers say they should?** [pause for answers]

---

## Slide 6 — Big is evil, small is beautiful (09:00–11:00)

Because it's tempting to think the answer is simple: small is good, big is evil. But watch what happens.

Node one, the rebel. A newcomer attacks the giant. That's Apple in 1984, casting IBM as Big Brother in its famous ad.

Node two, the giant. The rebel *becomes* the giant. By 2011, Motorola is running a Super Bowl ad casting *Apple* as Big Brother — the "white terror."

Node three, the new rebel. The next newcomer attacks, and the cycle restarts.

So good versus evil isn't a property of a company. It's a *position on a cycle*. Which means the real question is never "small or big." It's "what keeps power accountable?"

## Slide 7 — Part 2 divider (11:00–11:30)

Part two. Which brings us to the central question. If both faces are real — can a business be, on net, more good than bad? If yes, *how*? If no, *why not*?

---

## Slide 8 — The real question isn't "good or evil" (11:30–14:00)

I want to reframe the whole thing. The real question isn't "good or evil." It's whether, **on net**, the value a business creates outweighs the harm it pushes onto everyone else.

The case for yes: value creation is real — goods, jobs, innovation, a tax base. Harm is often a side-effect that regulation can actually price. And institutions can be redesigned, so business can be steered.

The case for no: the profit motive structurally pushes harm onto others. "Good" behavior is often just PR — greenwashing, ESG-washing. And bad actors outlast the regulation that's supposed to stop them.

Now the synthesis — and this is the idea I most want you to leave with: it's not good versus evil. It's **good versus badly governed**. The variable is accountability. Which makes business a *multiplier* — good or evil depending entirely on what it multiplies.

---

## Slide 9 — Four levers (14:00–17:00)

So what sets the sign on that multiplier? Four levers.

Competition. Monopoly removes the check that keeps prices and conduct honest — CNKI raising prices year after year, Monsanto locking farmers into patented seed.

Transparency. Sunlight is a disinfectant — but "Facebook has your data" shows how weak sunlight can be when nobody's really looking.

Regulation. Price a harm and the math changes; leave it unpriced and you're subsidizing bad behavior.

And norms and people — because individual choices still move the needle, and we'll come back to that.

The point: "more good than bad" is not a personality trait of a company. It's an *outcome* of the pressure system around it.

---

## Slide 10 — Part 3 divider (17:00–17:30)

Part three. And now it turns personal.

The moral lens meets the job offer. Will *you* weigh a company's CSR or ESG record when you choose where to work?

---

## Slide 11 — Does purpose move you? (17:30–20:00)

Here's the uncomfortable data. The most attractive employers for engineering students in China — Huawei, Tencent, Xiaomi, ByteDance, Alibaba, Apple, BYD, JD.com, Baidu, State Grid. And nearly nine in ten Gen Z say a company's purpose affects how long they'll stay.

But watch the gap. What we *say* — that purpose matters — and what we *do* — chase the highest offer. And notice: the most "attractive" employers are often the most contested — data, gig work, market power. So here's the honest question: **is "attractive" the same as "good"?** [pause]

---

## Slide 12 — Would you actually leave? (20:00–23:00)

The harder half. A company turns out to be socially irresponsible the week *after* you sign. Do you walk?

Reasons to stay: voice beats exit — you can reform from the inside. And let's be honest, you need the job; principle has a price too.

Reasons to leave: complicity has a cost — that OxyContin sales rep could have refused. Your labor *is* the product; you're funding the harm. And sunk cost is not a moral argument.

The honest middle, and my real advice: draw your line *before* the offer, not after. Try to leave the *room* before you leave the company — raise it first. And ask yourself what you'd want on your conscience in ten years.

Part four. Now cross to the other side of the desk. If you lead one day — profit, or purpose?

## Slide 14 — Profit — or something else? (23:30–27:00)

Two positions. The shareholder view, from Milton Friedman: maximize profit within the rules. That, he argued, is the only social responsibility of business.

The stakeholder view: the firm owes something to workers, customers, communities, and the future. Profit is a means, not the end.

And here's the nuance the debate usually misses: it's usually *not* a straight line. Firms that gut safety, labor, or the environment usually destroy value later. Purdue ended in bankruptcy and a seven-point-four-billion-dollar judgment.

So turn to the person next to you for a minute: **if you ran this company, would you take the profitable-but-harmful contract? Why — and how does your classmate answer differently?** [give 60–90 seconds, then take two answers]

---

## Slide 15 — When 'good' becomes the profitable move (27:00–29:00)

So is good just good business? Partly, yes.

Trust is an asset — brands that keep promises can charge a premium and retain talent. Hard to fake, hard to copy.

Cheap harm is short-lived harm — Purdue's "savings" became a seven-billion-dollar bill. Externalized cost eventually comes home.

But timing matters: a short-horizon leader can profit from harm for *years* before consequences arrive. So governance closes the gap — make the future cost visible *now*, through liability, disclosure, and competition.

Profit and purpose converge — but only when we stop letting firms borrow from the future.

---

## Slide 16 — Part 5 divider (29:00–29:30)

Part five. And I promise this connects.

Will there be shopping malls in the future? It sounds like trivia. Stay with me — it's deeper than it looks.

---

## Slide 17 — Will there be shopping malls? (29:30–33:00)

Quick vote — hands up: who thinks yes? Who thinks no? [count hands]

If yes — but transformed: not for buying things, e-commerce ate that. For *experience* — food, play, community, "third places." The mall becomes a stage for human presence, not a warehouse of goods. Who goes? People who are lonely for a place to *be*, not a thing to own.

If no — the institution dies: delivery, AR, social commerce dissolve the need. The mall was a child of car culture and cheap land — both changing. Ghost malls already dot the US and China. The function disperses; the building becomes a stranded asset.

Either way, notice what you're really answering: **is business a place we go, or a function that finds us?**

---

## Slide 18 — What we actually decided today (33:00–36:00)

Four things to take away.

One: business is a multiplier, not a moral agent. It amplifies whatever it's pointed at — and the pointing is human.

Two: good versus evil is a position on a cycle. Today's rebel is tomorrow's Big Brother. Vigilance, not romance.

Three: accountability, not virtue, decides the sign — competition, transparency, regulation, norms.

Four, and this is the one that lands on you: **your choices are the last lever.** The job you take, and the contract you refuse, are real votes.

---

## Slide 19 — Three questions to leave with (36:00–40:00)

Three questions to take out of this room.

One: pick one firm you admire — and then argue the opposite case honestly. What harm does its business model externalize?

Two: draw your line *now*. What would make you leave a job you love?

Three — the mall, but bigger: if business disappeared tomorrow, what part of human life would you miss most?

Thank you. The floor is yours — discussion is now open.
